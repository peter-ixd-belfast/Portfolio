Overview

same different is a brand, service and product offering, enabling adults, to explore their potential neurodiversity. It is designed to reduce the friction many adults are faced with when navigating the current pathway. This will enable waiting times for diagnosis to be significantly reduced and user satisfaction with the process to increase.

Role Brand, Service and Product Designer
Timeline 16 Weeks – (Jan – May 2026)
Audience Adults (over 18 years of age) who want to discover whether they are neurodiverse or not.
Tools & Methods User Research (Surveys and interviews), Desk Research (Scientific studies, personal blogs, etc.), Data Analysis, User Personas, User Journey Maps, Storyboarding, Service Design Blueprinting, Brand Design, Wireframing, Prototyping, User Testing (Moderated and Unmoderated, Remote and In-Person).
Outcome Service Design Blueprint validated with Autism Specialists and Support Workers.
Mobile App Prototype – Tested with 5 Participants.
Brand Guidelines Document.

The Problem

It is thought that over 500,000 adults in the UK are autistic but are unaware or waiting for an assessment. Waiting lists for assessments have reached 5 years in some parts of Northern Ireland with the average wait being 18 months. With NICE guidelines stating assessments should be carried out within 13 weeks of referral, the size of the issue is stark.

To access support requires a confirmed autism diagnosis. Therefore people waiting for an assessment cannot access support to help them understand their autism. Support can also allow autistic people to maximise their strengths and minimise the impact of any challenges they face.

The impacts of these waiting lists do not end with the individual, autism specialists face continuous pressure to carry out as many assessments as posible as well attend meetings, keep up-to-date with the most recent autism research, as well as ensure all administrative tasks are complete. Working in small, underresourced teams, these requirements are extremly difficult to meet and cause huge amounts of stress.

Requirements

  • A service design blueprint for a start-to-finish process allowing users to progress from questioning their neurodiversity to a diagnostic assessment with a specialist.
  • A brand that users can connect with and feel comfortable using while on their journey of discovery into their neurodiversity.
  • An interactive prototype of a digital product that enables a user to complete diagnostic tasks that do not require a human specialist.

Research & Discovery

I started this project with a period of research and discovery, part of this was to collate and analyse information from my own experience of the adult autism diagnosis pathway, as well as that I have gathered from conversations with professionals and other autistic people over many years.

I then supplemented my knowledge by carrying out two online surveys, which allowed to me gather a wide-ranges of qualatative and quantitive data from the perspective of both autism specialists and neurosiverse people.

Designing a new Adult Autism Diagnosis Service

After interpreting the data and uncovering key insights I was able to start the service design aspect of the project, I started by mapping out the current pathway and marking out the pain points and improvement opportunities.

This led to a process of ideation and iteration on a blueprint for a new service, until I had created a service design blueprint that stood up to the scuritiny of autism specialists and autisitc people.

Designing a Digital Product for the Service

With a service design in place, I was able to draw up a set of requirements of my digital product. With a clear set of requirements, I was able to create an early lo-fi design to test with a group of perspective users. While this proved that the product worked and met the requirements I had set, it did uncover several areas for improvement.

These user testing results allowed me to iterate on my design, and make some major changes.

These included:

  • A redesigned onboarding process, to reduce time to value for new users.
  • A new timeline based home screen, to allow users to see their whole journey mapped out, with clear indication of the work they had completed, what they needed to do next and what tasks lay ahead.
  • Using progressive disclosure to reduce cognitive load while not sacraficing important text content.
  • Refactoring the product's tone of voice, moving away from medical and deficency based terminology to a tone of voice with positivity, and support at its core.
  • The addition of animation and motion to celebrate a user's progress and help maintain motivation, on what can be a challenging journey.

With these changes made and a high-fidelity, interactive prototype created, I was able to complete a second round of user testing to verify the changes and iron out any final bugs. One issue that was discovered was the affordances I had included for clickable elements, which left users unsure as to which elements were interactive and which were not.

This required a quick redesign to ensure the difference was made clear and further testing proved the changes were successful. Final testing proved that the app met all of the requirements that I had set at the beginning of the project.

Designing a brand that users can connect with

The final part of the project was to design a brand that accuarately represented the values, vision and mission of the project. This was an ongoing task that I carried out alongside both the service and product design elements. I started by developing brand values, a vision for the brand, as well as a brand mission. These are key elements which shape the rest of the brand assets, as well as inform other design decisions, so I always endevaour to get these in place as early in a project as possible.

Brand Mission

Brand Mission

To bring together the best of human expertise and technology within a single service that supports autistic adults from initial concerns through to diagnosis. Treating people as individuals, and removing hurdles to accessing support are key facets of the service. Collaborative design and iteration based on user feedback are essential aspects from the beginning and will ensure accessibility across all aspects of the service. Ensuring people can understand and question how our technology is used will keep us on track and lead us to maximise our positive impact for everyone.

With my brand values, vision and mission in place, I could move on to developing visual elements for the brand. These include a brand logo, which turned out to be one of the most challenging aspects of the whole project. My research had shown that a lot of imagery and icons that have been assoicated with autism are controversial with autisitc people themselves, with a lot of autistic people feeling that this iconography has been assigned to them rather than being chosen by them.

I did not want my logo to have the same issues, so I spent a lot of time drafting different logo possibities, but even with refinement none of the options truly represented my brand in the way I wanted, in particular the individuality I wanted to represent for each individual user. A discussion with my lecturer Daniel, brought up the idea of a dyanamic brand, where one or more of the brand elements can change for individual users. Looking into this further it made sense for my logo to be different for each individual user.

Designing this dynamic logo, meant taking a different approach, where I needed to develop rules to guide the generation of each unique logo rather than simply designing a single logo. For this I decided to make the logo represent each user's individual journey, and as the journey takes ten steps from initial screener questionnaire to diagnosis decision, I developed a logo where 10 dots were connected by lines, similar to pictorial representations of star constellations. An algorithm could then place these dots and the connecting lines anywhere within a set bounding square and abibing by rules for minimum spacing between dots and that lines should not cross. I used this to develop my master logo which I used in all brand mock-ups and assets as an example.

I then applied the elements of the brand I had designed to the mobile app I had designed. This included using the dots from the logo on the home screen to represent each step and crafting an animation, so that as a user completed a task another completed dot and connecting line was added to their logo design.

Outcomes

Strenths and Weaknesses of the project

The key strength of this project, is that it looks at the problem of the high number of adults seeking an autism assessment, from the perspective of a person seeking an answer to whether they are autistic or not. While it is designed as a service to work alongside the current pathway, by changing the focus from what is available under current circumstances to what people actually need, this project illustrates, that many of ths issues we see within today's service could be reduced if not eliminated, by taking a user-centric approach and meeting people where they are. Every aspect of the design was considered from the perspective of an adult seeking answers and designed to eliminate the pain points with the current service that I identified through my research. This allowed me to present a complete service, product, and brand solution which had tested successfully with both potential users and professionals from the autism sector.

A weakness within the project is that refoctoring the service offering and developing a digital product, cannot overcome the issues of underfunding and a lack of trained professionals to offer autism assessments. While I can offer a product that can offer the ability to gave an accurate indcation of whether a person is autistic or not, only a trained professional can make a final diagnostic decision. This is to ensure elements that a digital product cannot recognise, such as failing to understand a question being asked or misrepresenting themselves can be assessed. This means I cannot claim my product will solve the complete issue of long assessment waiting lists, although it should help significantly.

Lessons Learned

A project of this length and scope has provided me with many lessons, the key one being the importance of user testing, by testing my project and brand regularly with a wide range of people, I was able not only to discover issues but also areas for improvement. This allowed me to create a significantly improved outcome than if I had have not completed testing. I feel another lesson I learned was the importance of setting strict scope parameters, in an area as large as autism diagnosis, with so many potential solutions, it would have been easy to allow scope creep to take over this project, and at the start I did set parameters that were simply not achieveable in the time I had available. I therefore had to cut certain aspects of my digital product to meet the deadlines that were in place. Unfortunately this did mean I had wasted time on the preparation work I had completed for these areas, and if I were to complete this project again, I would spend a little extra time at the start mapping out a realistic timeline to avoid wasting time looking into areas that I would not be able to complete work on.

Research & Discovery

My first discovery task was to validate my own knowledge of the adult autism diagnosis process as well as understand other people’s experiences of it. To achieve this, I created two surveys, one for adults who had completed the current pathway and a second for professionals who support neurodiverse adults. This allowed me to gain multiple different perspectives and helped me to prioritise the issues within the process.

Currently, there is no clear pathway for adults seeking a neurodiversity diagnosis; this leads to many people not knowing how to access the service.

Another purpose of these surveys was to discover people’s opinions on using AI within the diagnostic process. I had an idea that AI could offer several opportunities to streamline the diagnostic process; however, this would not be possible if users were not comfortable with AI. I therefore used the surveys to gather opinions on both how people felt generally about AI use and what safeguards they felt are required to ensure it was used ethically.

Survey participants were overwhelmingly positive about the potential of using AI within the diagnostic process, as long as its use was transparent and its purpose clear.

A key aspect of the research and discovery phase was talking to experts in Autism Diagnosis and support. While it was challenging to get time with such busy people and I had many disappointments with regards to people being unavailable. I was able to speak to two professionals, a support worker for autistic adults and the founder of a software company who had recently launched a tool to help autism diagnostic practitioners compile reports using AI. These discussions provided valuable insights that helped shape my initial ideation

While AI has a place within the diagnostic process, people were not comfortable with it making a final diagnostic decision; therefore, autism diagnostic specialists would still be a key part of the overall process.

Service Design

As my research and discovery made clear, a successful adult autism diagnostic service would need to combine technology (including AI) and human expertise. To be able to illustrate where these two aspects interacted, I decided a Service Design Blueprint would be a key artefact, I needeed to create.

My first step was to create a blueprint of the current process, to assess where the main pain points lay and what opportunities for improvement were available.

Significant parts of the current process are hidden from the user, leaving them with no idea at what stage they are, or how long the process may take.

I discovered that the vast majority of the pain points happened before a person sat down with a diagnostic specialist for an autism assessment. The work of the local health trust adult autism teams is highly regarded, especially considering the lack of resources they have. For this reason, I decided that the best approach was to design improvements that could be implemented without impacting the work of the specialists significantly.

I need to integrate my changes to the adult autism diagnostic process within the current overarching framework.

I could therefore concentrate on the initial stages of the process and develop a service design that utilised a web app to allow users to complete scientifically designed and verified questionnaires. AI could then score these questionnaires and calculate a person’s likelihood of autism. For those who were highly likely to be autistic, a direct referral from the app to the local health trust adult autism teams for assessment with a specialist could be made.

Moving the initial triage stage to an app would free up GP resources, which are currently overstreched.

I also wanted to bring a more human aspect to the project to avoid users getting lost in the artefacts, which, while familiar to designers, are not so to people who would be key stakeholders within the project.

Brand Design

While I envisaged my service being run by the NHS, a brand that users could connect with and trust was still vital to the overall success of the project.

I developed brand values, a mission as well as a brand vision to define the brand’s purpose and make it accountable for delivering its stated aims.

To innovate using the latest technology alongside human expertise to offer timely diagnosis and support for Autistic adults.

Many symbols used to denote autism are unpopular with autistic people, the autistic community often feel that these symbols have been assigned to them, rather than being something they can identify with on a personal level.

Create a brand that does not rely on common symbols associated with autism.

One aspect of being autistic is that while people may share a diagnosis, that does not impact their unique character and experience. For this reason, I wanted my brand to represent that individuality. This led me to explore dynamic branding, where aspects of the brand can change for each user. I decided that each user should have an individual brand logo, which was generated algorithmically when they signed up. This allowed me to state rules for the logo so they remained within the overall brand, but were still unique to each user.

Create a dynamic brand giving users a unique logo to represent their journey with the brand.

Another key aspect of my brand was the brand name, same different; this was designed to represent the juxtaposition of how we are all the same as humans but also different with unique characters and experiences. I also decided not to capitalise either word, as I didn’t want either to seem more important than the other.

Product Design

Completing my service design blueprint showed how much of my proposed adult autism diagnosis pathway was going to utilise a new mobile app. Therefore, I needed to design this app as part of the overall project. The purpose of the app was relatively straightforward: to allow users to complete a series of scientifically verified questionnaires in their own time. After each questionnaire, a user will receive an AI-generated summary of the results which will be easy-to-read without any medical jargon. Once a series of questionnaires have been completed, users will receive an indicated likelihood of autism, if this indicates a high probability of them being autistic, they will be referred for a specialist autism assessment with their local adult autism team.

My initial lo-fi designs followed this simple formula, and I completed a round of unmoderated user testing to get some initial feedback. The feedback from these tests showed, that while the app was functional, it took a long time to get any value from it. This led me to redesign the onboarding process, the first action was now to complete a screener questionnaire, rather than create an account. This would allow users to both experience the app and find out if it was going to be of value to them, before needing to provide any personal details.

Allow users to complete a screener questionnaire before creating an account to reduce time to value.

A key aspect of my product was allowing users to see their own personal diagnostic pathway. To achieve this, the main app home screen was set up as a timeline, showing clearly what step of the process the user was on, the steps they had completed and what steps they still needed to complete. This cured one of the biggest pain points I discovered in my research, where people were unclear on the process and where they were within it.

Centre the app experience around the overall process so that users always know what the process looks like and where they sit within it.

Another issue was that the app was very clinical in its approach. This apporach concentrates on a person’s deficits without acknowledging areas where they excel. This can lead to the process feeling quite negative, and lead to users losing motavation and not completing their journey. To combat this, I redesigned the AI-generated summaries after each questionnaire to contain both strengths and weaknesses. I also added tips to maximise these strengths and manage the weaknesses, which gave users added value.

Include the strengths that the person’s questionnaire answers indicate, not just the weaknesses.

I could now complete further user testing with my interactive prototype; this time, the tests were moderated, both in-person and remotely. They validated my overall design decisions and also allowed me to discover and subsequently correct some bugs within the prototype. This significantly improved the final outcome.

Completing multiple user tests allowed me to validate design decisions and fix minor bugs within the app.

Strengths

A key strength of this project is how it allows people to take control of their own journey, as they investigate whether they are autistic. Today, the process relies on other people, GPs, specialists, etc., and the person is never in control of what is going on; letters come through the post telling you what you need to do, often with a time limit, or when and where your appointment is. It is a very disjointed process, often with months of nothing happening followed by a burst of intense activity. This makes the process so much more difficult than it needs to be. same different will change this completely, allowing people to complete the journey in a way that works for them. They will know where they are in the process and what they need to do to reach the next stage. This knowledge and feeling of being in control will make a huge difference to people.

It also enables the NHS-provided service to run much more efficiently, firstly there will now be no need for people to have to go and speak to their GP, just to find out if an assessment would be useful. This will free up appointments as well as relieve the pressure on GPs to make decisions on people’s potential for being autistic that they have not had training to do. By digitising the standardised surveys, there will now not be the need for autism specialists to review and score the surveys manually, saving time and allowing them to carry out more valuable work. As same different’s AI model will be able to provide a specific clinician report of the questionnaires and results each individual person has completed, the time to prepare for each formal assessment will be reduced significantly, which may allow specialists the time to complete more assessments.

A small change, but one that makes a big difference, is the ability to book and manage an assessment appointment through the app. This will enable people to choose the date, time and appointment location that suits them. People who are neurodivergent often struggle with a range of challenges, and being able to select the best option for them will relieve a lot of stress and anxiety. Managing an appointment via the app will also help, as making phone calls causes many people stress, so removing the need for this will help make the process easier.

Weaknesses

The core weakness of the project is that it doesn’t tackle all the issues present in the current adult autism pathway. In fact, it uses a significant part of the existing system, which is overstretched and underfunded. There is no immediate solution to this beyond politicians supporting adult autism services with more funding, allowing more autism specialists to be employed.

Moving forward, the time savings offered to autism specialists could reduce the current cost of a formal assessment, allowing more people to utilise private healthcare providers. It could also allow more people to be assessed via the NHS for less funding, but this is not guaranteed.

Lessons Learned

This project taught me several lessons; the key one was how important user testing is. Going into the first round of testing, I was convinced the app I had designed was ideal; however, while functional, many areas needed significant improvement to make the app something people wanted to use.

I also learned that no matter how deeply you know the area you are working in (in this case, autism diagnosis) you still need to complete in-depth user research. My user research for this project uncovered additional pain points I was not aware of and allowed me to see the problem from several new perspectives, all of which contributed to a better final design.
